## The most specific findings

1. **Syllable-exact repeats** (Kotecha, CT-F5). When a melodic phrase repeats, the new line must match it syllable for syllable. If a chorus doesn't lift, move where the pre-chorus starts relative to beat one. This is directly usable as a check in the app.
2. **Stress matching** (Pattison, CT-F1). Stressed syllables go on strong beats. Content words take stress; articles, prepositions and pronouns don't. His bad example is a plea of "come back" set to a "bridegroom" rhythm. Coutts adds that pronouns and -ty/-ly endings are the syllables most often mis-stressed.
3. **Shape carries feeling** (Pattison, CT-F2). Even line counts, equal lengths and AABB/ABAB feel settled. Odd counts, a short or long last line and ABBA feel unsettled. Four tidy AABB lines about heartbreak "sound like facts".
4. **Exact spoken words** (Tranter, CT-F12; McAnally and Michaels, CT-F11). Write down what the artist actually says, keep their wording, and build poetic lines around it. Say it in a song the way you'd say it in conversation.
5. **The pre-chorus as cliffhanger** (Warren, CT-F6; Yacoub, CT-F7). The pre-chorus should leave the listener needing the chorus. Each section sits in its own vocal range. A chorus that doesn't land often has a pre-chorus problem.

## Where writers disagree

- **Melody first or lyric first?**
  - Sound or melody first: Dan Wilson ("look after the sounds and vowels"), Max Martin (picks the word that sounds best), Copperman/McAnally (words found inside sung syllables), Finneas on the Barbie song (chords, then melody, then improvised verse).
  - Lyric first: Lori McKenna says she starts from the lyric and asks what it wants to sound like.
  - Either or both: Wilson calls himself "title first" or "all at once"; Julia Michaels says it varies; Hillary Lindsey says it depends on the day.
  - Lyric-led in value: Finneas calls himself "a lyric person" and finds boring lyrics on a good melody less forgivable.
- **Rules versus feel.** Kotecha treats melody as exact "math". Michaels says she never thinks about a song's mathematics. Martin, Yacoub and Tedder sit in between: they write by feel and use the rules to diagnose problems.
- **How strict to be about rhyme.**
  - Pattison treats the rhyme type as an expressive choice: perfect rhyme closes a thought, assonance leaves it open.
  - Sloan notes identity and slant rhyme are normal in current rap and pop.
  - McAnally says "I love to rhyme" but feeling comes first.
  - Lindsey contrasts songs that come from the heart with "calculated" ones where you're working on a rhyme.
  - Not sourced: no fetched source says slant rhyme is "the modern default". Treat that idea as our assumption.
- **Plain words or unusual ones.** McAnally, Michaels and Tranter push for everyday speech. Finneas likes using words that "aren't used every day". Our reading is that these fit together: the syntax stays conversational while single word choices can be unusual.
