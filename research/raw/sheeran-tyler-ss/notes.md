## Most specific findings
1. Shape of You (F1): McDaid and Mac describe a fixed loop with melodies first sung in wordless syllables. Because the chords barely change, the sections differ through dynamics, lyric and rhythm. In separate testimony Sheeran calls it his own "phonetics", with the words following.
2. Writing roles (F2): McDaid calls Sheeran "macro" and himself "micro": Sheeran produces a flood of ideas and holds the whole song, while McDaid guards the precious few and works one section at a time.
3. Stick Season (F12–F14): Kahan sang the chorus in a borrowed twang so he could get "outside of" himself, and kept the verse's chords because a folk song should be simple. He feared the Vermont detail would alienate people. The literal gang-vocal last chorus was then judged a little corny.
4. Tyler (F11): he set a stated rule of "No intros, no outros, no bridges" for DON'T TAP THE GLASS, as a deliberate reaction against CHROMAKOPIA's heavy framing.
5. Tyler (F8–F9): press describes Hey Jane as two voices with the partner's reply landing harder, and Take Your Mask Off as stranger portraits that each end in the same address before the last verse turns on Tyler. Slant pushes back, calling the Hey Jane run sanitised and the mask portraits patronising.

## Variation within each group (not stereotypes)
- Sheeran: rhythm-first loop writing with a co-writer as editor (Shape of You), a fast single-room chord gift (Thinking Out Loud, ~20 min per Western Mail via Wikipedia), an idiom chosen because it matches his Irish trad roots (Azizam), and a song written alone from a found object (Old Phone).
- Tyler: a heavily framed persona record with an unexplained concept, against an explicitly unframed, sectionless and "silly" one. The humour versus vulnerability balance is praised by Treble and NME and attacked as evasive by Slant.
- Narrative group: Kahan built his song in public piece by piece; Boone joined two separate night ideas; Hozier rescued a preachy song by turning the chorus into a roll-call of names.
