## The most specific findings
1. **Beats first (F1).** Charlie Handsome says "Last Night" was written to an 8-bar voice-note loop. His aim is to make beats that already sound like hits, and he admits a hit hook can carry bad verses.
2. **A remark turned into a title (F4).** "I Had Some Help" came from Handsome's remark the night before, "we're going to need some help". Changing it to past tense and first person made it a confession. Walters says his steel-guitar part became the verse melody, and the song was written in about 24 minutes.
3. **The revealing detail (F9).** "Sangria" sat on the shelf for months until Osborne found one physical detail (he tastes it when he kisses her) that made a "girl's drink" title believable from a man.
4. **The idiom flip (F8).** In "When It Rains It Pours", a co-writer proposed turning a gloomy idiom upside down. In "Girl Crush", an admiring phrase is read as jealousy.
5. **Formula, admitted from inside (F11, F12).** McAnally called the bro-country trend "a rearranging of the same thing" that everyone writes, himself included. Gorley says big co-writing teams can have too many people in them and says writers think about radio too much. His advice: don't write a sequel to the artist's last hit.

## Genre convention vs general craft (what the evidence allows)
- **General craft:** flipping an idiom, finding the detail that makes the title true for the singer, singing a title out loud to test it, and throwing away a song that is only a "B".
- **Genre or industry convention:** the drinking, truck and small-town vocabulary; the persona who shifts the blame; spoken verses in the "talking country" tradition; and beat-first quick writes aimed at a set artist.
- **Where it turns into formula:** reviewers of the 37-track "I'm the Problem" call it repetitive (Paste: "more formulaic than totemic"). Chartmetric finds more writers per song go with more repeated words (correlation only). Wallen's own answer was to vary the sequencing ("two country, one cross"), not the lyrics.
