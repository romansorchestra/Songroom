## Most specific findings
1. **"the cure" (Song Exploder).** She threw out the first chorus but kept one image, and it became the outro. She couldn't write a wordy second verse, so she replaced it with one harsh line. Nigro held back the drums past the point where you expect them, so the payoff is "earned".
2. **"vampire" (GRAMMY.com, Nigro).** It started as a piano verse and chorus. She rejected his first ballad demo, so he moved it to double-time. The bridge came weeks later. Management said it sounded like "three songs in one", so they reworked the transitions. The tempo was eased because the demo felt rushed.
3. **"get him back!" (GRAMMY.com, Nigro).** She rejected the original verse. Weeks later he moved it into the bridge, where it worked.
4. **"lacy" (Tiny Desk).** It was the first song she wrote lyric-first, as a poem for a class. A Billboard critic praised how its chorus lyric changes each time it comes back.
5. **drivers license and the division of labour (Variety 2021, American Songwriter).**
   - Nigro hears drivers license as having no real chorus: a verse, then a build, then a big bridge. The verse was kept minimal and the bridge maximal.
   - She works to make every word land, and he fixes stanzas that have too many or too few words.

## Variation across the sample (beyond the stereotype)
The songs do not share one method:
- a ballad that turns into a "rock opera" (vampire)
- a joke premise with mostly spoken verses and chorus and a sung pre-chorus (bad idea right?)
- a two-way idiom as the title (get him back!)
- a lyric written first as a poem and left ambiguous (lacy)
- a counter-reply to another writer's line (the grudge)
- an open question asked from inside a happy relationship (the cure)
