# Eight songs, examined whole (19 Oct 2026, commission Phase C)

The first round (10 Oct) collected writers' accounts of their process but examined no song as a whole work. This round examined eight, two per slice, under `research/raw/SONG_ANALYSIS_STANDARDS.md`: legitimate access only, no lyric stored beyond a phrase of six words or fewer, no claim about the recording (there was no audio), every fetched text labelled as tool-extracted. Each record answers the commission's ten questions (angle, narrator, section map, title/hook, language choices, connective lines, musical boundary, writer commentary vs inference, transferable craft and its limits, one original exercise). Raw records: `research/raw/songs-*/`; staged with sources, findings, cards and profiles in `research/pack/candidates/` (not loaded by the app).

## Blocked or not used

Musixmatch and LyricFind (the licensed displays) refused the fetch tool for every song; no artist site printed lyrics; no route around a refusal was tried. Unlicensed lyric sites were not opened. Two Billboard pages, the WSJ and a Tennessean piece could not be fetched. Lyric descriptions that rest on a press page printing the lyric without a stated licence are labelled as such in every record; drop them if that access is judged not good enough, and the writer statements still stand.

## The most useful distinctions (staged as cards C62–C68, not live)

1. **Move the chorus off the narrator** (People Watching; a documented rewrite). The chorus that stalled summed up what had happened to him; the one that worked watches strangers and names one of them as somebody's loved one. Someone hearing only the chorus wouldn't know anyone had died. It is a documented counterexample to C43 ("the chorus should tell the whole song"), which now carries it as a limit.
2. **One small qualifier carries the confession** (Someone Like You, the co-writer's account). The chorus states the brave claim and one word turns it into the opposite admission, so it can be sung straight.
3. **A title that is an unfinished sentence whose ending changes** (Something in the Orange): the same frame gives a hopeful verdict at the end of a verse and the final one at the end of each chorus.
4. **Polite to blunt inside one chorus** (Please Please Please): the sweet title first, the petty stake next, the bluntest word last.
5. **Open a loss song through grammar, not vagueness** (Someone You Loved): past tense, "you", the narrator as the one who was loved, so it fits a breakup or a death without going generic.

## What did not become a card, and why

The gendered-word revision (one case, narrow), the moving image in Something in the Orange (low confidence; the two descriptions disagree), and two musical points (a key change marking a fantasy verse; setting a grief song to where it ends up, not to its subject): kept as findings, because the writer can't use them on words.
